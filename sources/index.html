How this archive reads evidence

Follow the claim to its source.

Historical statements are linked to the records that support them. Separate comparative references inform the new fictional canon; they do not certify its rules or give them force beyond the fiction.

Comparative constitutional and human-rights sources

These official or authoritative texts inform general design principles only. They do not establish Bhoomi law, statehood, recognition, or treaty obligations. No foreign constitution is adopted wholesale. Treaty duties apply to the relevant States Parties, not to this fictional project.

  1. C1

    Germany’s Basic Law · official English text

    Scope: Rights and remedies (Articles 1, 19), federal responsibilities (30, 70), constitutional amendment limits (79), constitutional review (93), judicial independence (97), and emergency provisions (115a–115l).

    Finding: Rights bind public power; federal responsibilities and residual authority are allocated between the Federation and Länder; some constitutional principles are protected from amendment.

    Limit: Comparative reference only. The English version is for convenience; the German text controls. These German rules are not Bhoomi law.

  2. C2

    South African Constitution · Department of Justice chapter index

    Relevant chapters: Bill of Rights (sections 7–39, including rights limits and emergencies), co-operative government (40–41), independent institutions (181–194), and Parliament and amendments.

    Finding: National, provincial and local spheres are distinctive, interdependent and interrelated; independent oversight institutions act without interference and report to the National Assembly.

    Trade-off and limit: Cooperative spheres are not the same design as a federation, and South Africa’s own allocation and priority rules are not Bhoomi law.

  3. C3

    Canada’s Constitution Acts and Charter of Rights and Freedoms

    Scope: Federal and provincial responsibilities, rights limits and remedies, amendment procedures, and Charter section 33.

    Finding: Canada combines constitutional division of powers with rights review. Section 33 permits a time-limited legislative override for specified guarantees, renewable every five years.

    Trade-off and limit: The override gives elected legislatures room to act but lets some rights protections be set aside. It is one comparator, not a recommendation or Bhoomi law.

  4. C4

    International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights · OHCHR text

    Scope: Effective remedies (Article 2), fair hearing (14), expression (19), public participation (25), emergency derogations (4), federal application (50), and reporting (40).

    Finding: Emergency derogations are constrained; certain rights may not be suspended, and a person must have an effective remedy for violations.

    Limit: This is a treaty, not a complete domestic government design. Its obligations apply to States Parties and are not represented as Bhoomi obligations.

  5. C5

    Convention on the Rights of the Child · OHCHR text

    Scope: Child nationality and identity safeguards (Articles 7–8), the child’s views (12), non-discrimination (2), and best interests (3).

    Finding: Children’s identity, nationality and participation deserve explicit protection in public systems that affect them.

    Limit: The Convention binds its States Parties; it does not supply a complete citizenship code or create a Bhoomi treaty obligation.

  6. C6

    Convention on the Reduction of Statelessness · OHCHR text

    Scope: Nationality safeguards for people who would otherwise be stateless (Articles 1–2), limits on loss or deprivation (7–9), and dispute settlement (14).

    Finding: Nationality rules can include safeguards for children born stateless and foundlings, and restrict nationality loss where it would cause statelessness.

    Limit: This treaty has specified rules and conditions for contracting States. It is not a universal citizenship code or a Bhoomi obligation.

Design tension: Federal autonomy can conflict with national standards; stronger emergency review reduces executive discretion but may slow crisis response; supermajority and referendum rules protect continuity but make amendments harder; independent appointments limit capture but may be slow, especially before the Council’s future member units are defined.
