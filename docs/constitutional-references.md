# Constitutional design references

These primary texts offer comparative evidence for a later fictional constitutional design. They are not templates; treaty obligations apply to their states parties, and no foreign model is presented as the answer for Bhoomi. No new Bhoomi constitutional, government, legal, citizenship, territorial, or officeholder facts are drafted here.

## What the sources show

- [Germany’s Basic Law (C1)](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/englisch_gg/englisch_gg.html), Articles 1(3), 19, 30, 70, 79, 93, 97 and 115e–115l, provides examples of directly binding rights, powers retained by Länder unless assigned to the Federation, a constitutional court, judicial independence, a heightened amendment rule with entrenched principles, and defined defence-emergency authorities. The official English translation is for convenience; the German text controls.
- [South Africa’s Constitution (C2)](https://www.justice.gov.za/legislation/constitution/saconstitution-web-eng.pdf), sections 7–8, 36–38, 37, 40–41, 74 and 181–182, describes governmental spheres as distinct, interdependent and interrelated; pairs rights with a reasoned limits test and court remedies; creates independent oversight bodies with annual reporting duties; and sets time limits and renewal safeguards for emergencies. It is not labeled a federation in the cited text, and national-prevalence rules constrain some provincial autonomy.
- [Canada’s Constitution Acts (C3)](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/const/), Constitution Act, 1867 sections 91–92 and 95, and Constitution Act, 1982 sections 1, 24, 33, 38, 41 and 52, show exclusive and concurrent powers, judicial remedies, constitutional supremacy, subject-specific amendment thresholds, and a limited, time-bounded legislative override for specified Charter rights.
- The [International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (C4)](https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/international-covenant-civil-and-political-rights), Articles 2(3), 4, 14, 19, 25, 40 and 50, addresses effective remedies, independent and impartial tribunals, emergency derogations subject to strict necessity and non-derogable rights, political participation, reporting, and application throughout federal states. It is a treaty, not a domestic constitution, and does not prescribe a complete internal powers map or a domestic oversight agency.
- The [Convention on the Rights of the Child (C5)](https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/convention-rights-child), Articles 7–8 and 12, connects birth registration, nationality, identity preservation and children’s participation, with explicit attention to the risk of statelessness. The Convention relies on implementation through national law and procedure and is not a federal powers model.
- The [1961 Convention on the Reduction of Statelessness (C6)](https://legal.un.org/ilc/texts/instruments/english/conventions/6_1_1961.pdf), Articles 1–2, 7–9 and 14, contains targeted safeguards concerning children who would otherwise be stateless, foundlings, nationality loss and deprivation. It also permits defined conditions and exceptions and is a treaty for its contracting states, not a universal citizenship code.

## Trade-offs to resolve in a later design

- **Enumerated versus residual powers:** enumerated allocations can clarify responsibility; residual powers determine where unlisted matters fall. Concurrent fields enable cooperation but need rules for disputes and conflicting laws.
- **Rights and limits:** directly enforceable rights and remedies protect individuals. Proportionate limitation tests allow rights to be balanced; broad overrides add legislative flexibility but reduce predictability and may weaken safeguards.
- **Judicial and institutional independence:** multi-institution appointments, protected tenure and independent budgets can reduce direct political control. Appointment deadlocks, capture risks and democratic accountability still need treatment.
- **Amendment thresholds:** supermajorities, regional assent and referendums protect foundational commitments through broad agreement. The higher the threshold, the easier it may be for a minority to block change.
- **Emergency authority:** fast, concentrated powers may help in a crisis. Short sunsets, renewal by legislatures, public reasons, judicial review and non-derogable rights constrain abuse but can slow action.
- **Public accountability:** open proceedings, public reports, audit and access-to-records rules aid scrutiny. Privacy, security and fair-process interests may require narrow exceptions and independent review.
- **Citizenship:** predictable rules for birth, descent and naturalisation support administration. Eligibility conditions and deprivation powers can exclude people; safeguards against statelessness and access to review constrain that risk.

These are design questions, not decisions about Bhoomi’s future institutions or laws. The original 2025 sources remain the only evidence for the historical claims they contain; new canon requires a separate, clearly dated project decision.
